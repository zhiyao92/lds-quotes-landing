Gratitude in ordinary moments

Notice a gift without grading the whole day.

Gratitude can begin with something specific: a skill that helped another person, or a moment when someone made time to listen. These two readings explore receiving a gift and remembering a kindness. One is an original reflection; the other pairs an excerpt from Henry B. Eyring’s “O Remember, Remember” with separate commentary.

The focus is attention, not comparison. A helpful moment can be worth keeping even when other parts of the day were painful or unresolved. Read at your own pace. If nothing comes to mind, you can leave the question open and return another time.

Receive a kind word

Someone thanks you for a lesson, a song, or a skill you shared. Before the words have settled, you hear yourself say, “It was nothing.”

Humility need not require denying a gift. There can be room to thank Heavenly Father for what you have been given and also accept that it helped another person. A simple “Thank you; I am glad it was useful” leaves the gift intact without making it a competition.

Which gift are you learning to receive and share more comfortably?

Conference excerpt + reflection · First shared 5 November 2026

Remember a kindness

LDS Quotes reflection

When a day ends with unfinished tasks, its quieter moments can be easy to overlook. Henry B. Eyring’s “O Remember, Remember” invites us to recognize and remember God’s kindness.

One way to understand that invitation is as a record, not a scorecard. A note about someone listening can preserve something meaningful without declaring that everything turned out well. Hardship and gratitude do not have to cancel each other out, and a difficult day does not require a positive summary.

If you’d like, what ordinary kindness would you choose to remember from this week?

Keep a small, honest record

If you would like to write something down, start with what happened: who listened, what was offered, or which gift you were able to share. Add why it mattered to you. There is no need to turn the entry into a verdict about the day or to share a private experience publicly. A sentence can be enough.
