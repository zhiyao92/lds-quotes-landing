How to turn saved LDS quotes into a small weekly habit

A simple routine for revisiting favorite LDS quotes, checking their original context and choosing one thoughtful action.

By Kelvapp · 7 November 2026 · 3 min read

Saving an inspiring quote is easy. Returning to it is the part that tends to disappear somewhere between the next notification and the laundry. Before long, your favorites can become a very uplifting storage cupboard.

You do not need a complicated study system to make saved words useful. A small weekly routine can give them a place in everyday life. LDS Quotes provides topics, authors, favorites, reminders and shareable cards; the habit below is something you can build around those tools.

Save with a reason

When a quote stands out, pause before adding it to favorites. Ask yourself why you want to return to it. Perhaps it raises a question, describes a quality you want to practice or reminds you of a conversation that deserves more care.

You do not have to turn every reading moment into homework. A short mental note is enough. If you want a written reflection, use a notebook or another place you already keep personal notes. The purpose is to remember why these particular words mattered to you.

Choose one place to begin

Browse a topic or author in LDS Quotes instead of trying to cover everything at once. If you are unsure what to read, For You uses three questions to suggest a selection. Its preview is available to explore, while saving personalized lists to your account requires Premium.

Favorites and personalized lists serve different purposes. For this routine, you only need a few individual passages you intend to revisit. A growing collection is optional; paying attention to one passage is enough for a session.

Set a realistic return point

Pick a weekly moment you can recognize, such as after a regular personal-study session. Put a reminder in your calendar if that helps. The app also offers daily reminders and Home Screen widgets, which you can use as gentle prompts if they suit your day.

A prompt should make returning easier, not create another obligation. If a daily notification becomes background noise, reconsider when you read. Missing a day does not require a catch-up marathon or a dramatic conversation with your streak counter.

Read beyond the card

At your weekly return point, choose one saved quote and look for its original source. Read the surrounding passage before using it in a talk, lesson or message. A short excerpt may leave out the question, audience or circumstances that explain what the speaker meant.

The Church’s official General Conference library is a useful place to find conference addresses. If you cannot verify a quotation’s source, avoid presenting an uncertain attribution as settled fact. It is fine to keep a question open while you look further.

Choose a response that fits your life

After reading, ask: “What do I want to consider or do differently?” Your response could be a private reflection, another question to study or a small act of consideration. You do not need to extract a universal lesson or make a public declaration.

Keep the action modest and specific. For example, you could decide to listen without preparing your reply during one conversation. That is a personal practice you choose, not a promise that a quote will solve a difficult relationship.

Share thoughtfully, or keep it private

LDS Quotes lets you customize and share quote cards. Before sending one, check the source and think about the recipient. Would the message feel welcome, or might it sound like advice they did not ask for? Sometimes your own kind sentence is more helpful.

LDS Quotes is an independent app, not an official Church publication. Use official sources for authoritative guidance. For your next small study session, open LDS Quotes, revisit one favorite and give it a little attention. You can leave the rest of the cupboard for another day.
